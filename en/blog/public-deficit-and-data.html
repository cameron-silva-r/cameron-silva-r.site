Public deficit and data: what the figures actually measure

Before commenting on a public deficit, it helps to know exactly which figure is being discussed: the headline balance or the structural balance, a Eurostat notification or cash-based Treasury accounting, a provisional estimate or a revised figure. These distinctions often change the diagnosis more than the political debate itself.

The most widely quoted deficit figure is the headline general government balance as a share of GDP, defined under the European System of Accounts (ESA 2010) and notified twice a year to Eurostat as part of the excessive deficit procedure. It aggregates central government, local government and social security, and it includes items that are not cyclical at all: interest payments on existing debt, but also one-off operations (bank recapitalisations, litigation settlements, exceptional transfers) that can swing the balance from one year to the next without saying much about the underlying trajectory.

This is exactly why institutions distinguish the headline balance from the structural balance, which strips out the effect of the business cycle (lower tax receipts in a downturn, higher social spending) as well as one-off and temporary measures. The structural balance is closer to what one actually wants to assess: the sustainable stance of fiscal policy. Its major weakness, well documented in public finance research, is that it relies on an estimate of potential GDP that is not directly observable, changes with methodological revisions, and can therefore be revised significantly after the fact, even for years that are already closed.

A second pitfall concerns the frequency and nature of the data. Where quarterly public finance figures exist, they are not always seasonally adjusted or directly comparable across countries, unlike harmonised annual notifications. Comparing a monthly Treasury cash balance with a nationally-accounted notified balance, or a provisional figure with a final one published a year later, leads to misreadings that nonetheless circulate widely in public debate.

Finally, statistical revisions are themselves informative. The first deficit and debt estimates, published the following spring, are routinely revised in the autumn and again in subsequent years, sometimes by several tenths of a percentage point of GDP, as administrations gain access to more complete tax and accounting data. Tracking a fiscal indicator over time therefore means tracking its revisions too, not just its level at the time of publication.
