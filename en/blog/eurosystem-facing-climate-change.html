The Eurosystem facing climate change

The European Central Bank's mandate remains price stability, but climate change is increasingly part of the picture, both as a macroeconomic risk and as a genuine monetary policy topic in its own right.

Climate change affects price stability through two channels the ECB has explicitly distinguished since launching its climate action plan in 2021: physical risk, linked to extreme weather events that disrupt agricultural output, energy supply or supply chains and feed inflation volatility; and transition risk, linked to the cost of shifting to a low-carbon economy, which can weaken entire sectors (fossil fuels, carbon-intensive industries) and, through them, the balance sheets of the banks financing them.

On this basis, the Eurosystem has adjusted several operational tools: climate stress tests have been run on both banks' balance sheets and its own; the corporate bond purchase programme (CSPP) has been "tilted", with reinvestments directed toward issuers with better climate performance; and climate-related disclosure requirements have gradually been folded into the eligibility criteria for collateral accepted in refinancing operations.

These choices have revived an older doctrinal debate over the "market neutrality" principle: should a central bank purchase assets in proportion to their market weight, without sectoral distinction, or can it legitimately tilt purchases toward objectives deemed to serve the public interest without overstepping its primary mandate? Supporters of greening argue that climate risk is already a conventional financial and macroeconomic risk, and therefore legitimately taken into account; critics see a risk of disguised industrial policy that could pull the ECB onto more political terrain than monetary policy proper, setting a precedent that could extend to other priorities.

This debate is not unique to the euro area: other central banks (the Bank of England, the Federal Reserve) have taken noticeably different positions, some more cautious about using asset-purchase tools for climate purposes. It illustrates a broader tension facing contemporary central banks: as their toolkit expands (asset purchases, collateral rules, supervision), the line between monetary policy in the strict sense and more structural choices about credit allocation becomes harder to draw.
