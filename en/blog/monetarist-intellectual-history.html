An intellectual history of monetarism

A minority current against the dominant postwar Keynesian orthodoxy, monetarism left a deep mark on macroeconomic thought and central bank practice, long after its strictest tenets fell out of use.

Monetarism took shape at the University of Chicago in the 1950s and 1960s, driven by Milton Friedman, around a simple but then heterodox claim: "money matters". By reviving the quantity theory of money, Friedman and his colleagues argued that a stable long-run relationship exists between money supply growth and inflation, and that short-run economic fluctuations are largely explained by unanticipated changes in that money supply.

This claim stood in direct opposition to the then-dominant Keynesian view, which emphasised aggregate demand and justified discretionary fiscal and monetary fine-tuning of the business cycle. Friedman countered this confidence in fine-tuning with his famous "long and variable lags" argument: the effects of monetary policy actions materialise with an uncertain and variable delay, making fine-tuned management of the cycle not only difficult but potentially destabilising. From this he drew a policy conclusion that was radical for its time: a simple, constant-rate money growth rule (the "k-percent rule") is preferable to discretionary management, however well-intentioned.

These ideas found a dramatic practical application at the turn of the 1980s, when Paul Volcker at the Federal Reserve, followed to varying degrees by the Bundesbank and the Bank of England, adopted monetary aggregate growth targets to break inflation that was seen as out of control. The Volcker episode in particular left a lasting mark: disinflation was achieved, but at the cost of a dramatic rise in interest rates and a severe recession, which fuelled a lasting debate over the social cost of disinflation policies.

Strict monetarism nonetheless declined fairly quickly as an operational doctrine: financial deregulation and innovation in payment instruments made money demand unstable and monetary aggregates increasingly unpredictable, depriving central banks of a reliable anchor. Most abandoned strict monetary targeting during the 1990s in favour of inflation targeting. Monetarism's intellectual legacy nonetheless persists within these newer frameworks: the idea that a credible, announced rule beats unpredictable discretion, the emphasis placed on anchoring expectations, and a wariness of short-run cyclical activism all remain, in a different form, at the heart of contemporary central banking practice.
